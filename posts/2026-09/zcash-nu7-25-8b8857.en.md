On September 14, the Zcash community published the results of its NU7 upgrade vote. Among the choices: keep 25-second block times, preserve the halving schedule. It reads like a technical footnote. It is not. The vote is a live experiment in something most blockchain projects still handle badly—deciding, in public, what a network will become, and then living with that decision permanently.

## The Halving Is a Commitment Device, Not a Feature

Zcash’s halving—cutting block rewards roughly every four years—is often described as a scarcity mechanism. The more interesting property is that it is a promise the network cannot easily break. Changing it would require a coordinated upgrade, and the NU7 vote shows the community chose not to. That choice matters because Zcash’s privacy guarantees depend on miners having a predictable incentive to keep securing the chain. A 25-second block time, meanwhile, is not just faster UX. It changes the orphan rate, the mempool dynamics, and how much time shielded transactions spend in flight. The community looked at those trade-offs and chose continuity.

This is the unglamorous work of protocol governance: not picking the most exciting option, but picking the one you can defend five years from now.

## Governance Is Only Credible If the Record Survives

Votes like NU7 are only meaningful if the outcome is verifiable later. If a future developer can quietly reinterpret the vote, or if the discussion history disappears, the governance was theater. Zcash has an advantage here—its forum threads, ZIP proposals, and vote tallies are public. But “public” on the conventional web is not the same as “durable.” Links rot. Forums get migrated. Companies that host community infrastructure get acquired or shut down.

This is not a hypothetical. In 2021, when the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine faced a major outage, countless citations in technical discussions went dark. For a protocol like Zcash, where a single ZIP document can determine how shielded pools behave, losing the historical record is not an inconvenience—it is a governance failure. The same logic applies to any organization making long-lived technical decisions: the reasoning behind a choice is as important as the choice itself.

## The Storage Bill for “Permanent” Decisions

Here is where the economics get uncomfortable. Storing governance records, audit trails, and protocol specifications indefinitely is cheap right now because someone else is paying—usually through a subscription. But subscriptions assume the payer keeps paying. A 2019 study by the think tank Internet Memory found that the average lifespan of a web page is about 100 days; for corporate documents, it is longer but still finite. When a cloud provider raises prices or shuts a region, the data does not vanish immediately, but the commitment does.

Arweave’s model flips this: you pay once, and the storage fee is endowed to cover future costs. That makes it suited to records that must outlive the organization that created them. Long Drive, a permanent-storage drive built on Arweave and operated by Supercomputing International in Hong Kong, applies this to everyday files—corporate archives, legal documents, long-term media. It is not a replacement for a hot cloud drive; it is a different category of tool, for the subset of data you never want to re-upload.

## What NU7 Actually Teaches

The Zcash vote is not a story about 25 seconds. It is a story about a community accepting that its decisions have long tails. The halving schedule will shape miner economics for a decade. The block time will shape user experience for just as long. And the vote record itself will be cited by developers who were not in the room.

That is the real question for any system that claims to be durable: not whether it can store data, but whether it can store intent. A blockchain can preserve a transaction forever. It can also preserve the reasoning behind a protocol change—if someone chooses to write it down and pay for the space. The NU7 vote is a reminder that permanence is not just a storage property. It is a governance one.
