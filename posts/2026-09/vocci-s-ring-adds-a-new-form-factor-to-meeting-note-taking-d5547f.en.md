Vocci's new meeting ring costs $249. For that price, you get a lightweight band that listens, transcribes, and files away what was said in the room. TechCrunch's coverage notes the obvious tension: a device designed to remember everything raises privacy questions that a price tag cannot answer.

The ring is not the interesting part. The interesting part is what it implies about how we now treat conversation. A meeting used to end when the room emptied. Now it ends when the storage fills up—or, more precisely, when someone decides the record is no longer worth keeping. Vocci is selling memory as a feature. But memory, in software, is never neutral. It is an index, a search result, a liability, and an asset all at once.

## The economics of remembering

Consider the subscription math. Most meeting-note tools charge monthly, per seat. A team of ten on a $15-per-user plan spends $1,800 a year to keep transcripts and summaries available. Over five years, that is $9,000—roughly the cost of a used car—for records that may or may not survive the next pricing change, acquisition, or shutdown.

That is not a hypothetical. When a note-taking startup pivots or gets acquired, the archive often becomes a migration problem. Users export ZIP files. Some files open. Some do not. The meeting where you agreed on the Q3 budget becomes a folder of JSON that no current app can render.

The ring adds a hardware layer to this fragility. Hardware fails, batteries degrade, firmware stops updating. A $249 device that records your meetings is also a $249 device that can stop working. The transcript may live in the cloud, but the cloud is a subscription, not a guarantee.

This is where permanent storage stops being an abstract ideal and becomes a practical question. If a meeting record matters—a board decision, a client agreement, a design review—it should not depend on a vendor's continued interest in the category. It should be a file you own, stored somewhere that does not require a monthly fee to stay alive. Long Drive, built on Arweave, was designed around that premise: upload once, pay once, and the data persists on a decentralized network without a renewal cycle. It is a narrow use case, not a replacement for every app, but it is the right shape for records that need to outlast the tools that created them.

## Privacy is not a setting

The ring's privacy problem is not that it records. It is that recording is the default, and forgetting is the exception. In most workplaces, a spoken aside is ephemeral by social contract. A ring on a finger changes that contract without asking the room.

Vocci can add indicators, consent flows, and local-only modes. Those help. But the deeper issue is architectural. If the transcript is encrypted with a key the vendor holds, privacy is a policy. If it is encrypted with a key the user holds, privacy is a property. The difference matters when the vendor changes its terms, suffers a breach, or simply decides to train a model on your meetings.

Client-side encryption—where the password protects the private key and even the platform cannot read the file—turns a promise into a constraint. It is not a feature you toggle for convenience. It is the only version of "private" that survives a subpoena, an acquisition, or a bad quarter.

## The archive problem

Meeting notes are a small slice of a larger pattern. We now generate more records than any previous era—transcripts, photos, contracts, medical scans, family videos—and we store most of them on services that assume continuous payment and continuous existence.

The result is a quiet form of loss. Not deletion, exactly. Just decay: files that no longer open, accounts that lapse, formats that fall out of support. The cloud was supposed to solve this. Instead it moved the problem from hardware failure to business failure.

A $249 ring is a useful reminder that capture is cheap and preservation is not. The hard part is not recording the meeting. It is making sure the record is still there—readable, intact, and under your control—when the meeting, the ring, and the company that made it are all gone.

That is the standard worth holding new tools to. Not whether they can remember, but whether they can be trusted with what they remember.
